TCI Turns 10

Dear reader,

The Creative Independent is now ten years old. That’s a little over a quarter of the Web’s entire history. It’s wild to think how much and how little everything has changed. And many artists are dealing with the same questions: how to stay productive in precious times, how to juggle a creative practice and day job, how to work through creative blocks…

To mark it, we did something we had never done: we read the whole thing at once. Not skimmed. Read, and counted. Every “recommends” list. Every mention of a day job, a kid, a block, a loom. We wanted to know what 2,132 conversations actually add up to when you put them side by side.

What follows is the report, more or less as it came out. We’ve left it in its working clothes.

How this was done — and what the numbers mean

Source: every interview in the archive — 2,132 published interviews with ~2,095 distinct people. 23 posts marked unpublished (a test post, old drafts, and interviews scheduled for Sep–Oct 2026) are excluded. Counts are of distinct people: someone interviewed twice counts once, and in joint interviews the specific person is named.

Three kinds of evidence were used. (1) Each interview’s “recommends” list, read in full. (2) TCI’s own vocation and tag fields. (3) The interviews themselves: answers were narrowed by keyword to relevant passages, then classified against a written rule for each question, and a sample of every question was checked by hand, with manual corrections where the rule was misapplied. The interviewer’s questions never count as evidence, except where the interviewer states a plain fact about the interviewee (“you’re also a father”).

Counts from recommendation lists and vocations are close to complete. Counts that come from conversation — parents, day jobs, late starters, hobbyists, and teachers beyond those whose vocation says so — are lower bounds: someone who never mentions their kids in a way the search catches isn’t counted.

For the advice questions, the number beside each theme is how many interviewees gave that advice among those who talked about the topic. The ranking is the finding; the absolute numbers depend on how the passages were narrowed. A handful of advice-givers (at most three per topic) came from the 23 unpublished posts; they’re removed from the totals, so individual theme counts may be off by one or two.

On parenting — 242 people

Rule: says they have children (any age, biological, adoptive or step). Since everyone interviewed has a working practice, every parent counts as a working parent. 176 of the 242 talk specifically about fitting parenting around their work — time, money, energy, how kids changed the practice. Lower bound.

All those years of struggling in New York, and going to work and coming back home and working on music after putting my daughter to bed and countless hours of rehearsal...You realize that you have it, but you only really discover it through putting yourself through it. There’s no other way to know. It’s that struggle and pain. It’s a cliché, but it’s true.

– Raquel Berríos

But, I decided I needed to really focus on my artwork right now, and so I have Monday and Tuesday fully devoted to the artwork. Wednesday is a hybrid day. Thursday and Friday, fully devoted to the farm, and then Saturday is for [my son] Cyrus, and always the farm and then some art. That’s how my week is really spent these days. Basically, I’m making paintings for the first time for this show, and also making a video.

– Agathe Snow

Also, being a parent, and I’ve said this out loud during conventions, that I cannot stress how important having a child was to my career because everything I’d done before them was like, “Yeah, I’ll go to work, and I’ll earn a paycheck, and I’ll come home, and then I’ll have fun, and I’ll play World of Warcraft, or I’ll play role playing games on the weekend with my friends, or whatever, and I’ll occasionally design a game.

– Patrick Leder

Lots of stuff about being a parent-being a parent is great, but it also sucks. There was a lot of stuff about alcohol. You know, it’s a destroyer and it’s a preserver, literally. Alcohol is what led me to realize, like, “Right, Destroyer and Preserver.“ Almost all of the poems had that kind of two-faced thing to them, so that’s how I got to that, and once I had that title, I was like, “Right.

– Matthew Rohrer

On working day jobs — 109 people now; 281 have held one

Rule: holds a job outside their creative practice to support it — waiting tables, retail, office work, teaching done to pay the bills, and so on. A job that is the career they’re interviewed about doesn’t count. 109 had one at the time of their interview; 167 had one earlier; the rest of the 281 had one at an unclear time. Lower bound.

I work one day a week and every holiday still, as a floor cleaner, to make my stable living. I’ve been blessed to be able to support my practice through painting sales. I work five or six days a week as a painter here in my studio. I’m still figuring out how that works. So far it’s going well. I can live for my paintings, and that feels really amazing.

– Menno Pasveer

I’m a college professor. I teach film production and film studies at Bard College. I, for better or worse, don’t really separate my day job from my art practice. – Ephraim Asili

I’m going to watch every movie of that genre.“ Or every movie with a dentist character, or whatever.

3. What’s the most common advice for…

On creative block — 265 people gave advice on this

Most common: change what you’re doing, or where you’re doing it — switch medium, project or room rather than staring at the same wall. The next two answers pull in opposite directions and are nearly tied: step away and trust it will come back, or push through on a schedule.

  • 1.Change what you’re doing, or where you’re doing it46
  • 2.Step away, rest, and trust the timing40
  • 3.Push through with discipline and routine35
  • 4.Feed the well — read, watch, look at other people’s work33
  • 5.Diagnose the real problem (it’s often not a “block”)31
  • 6.Lower the stakes — make something deliberately bad23
  • 7.Let go of conscious control (chance, Oblique Strategies)23
  • 8.Move your body23
  • 9.Reframe the block as normal or temporary22
  • 10.Protect your headspace from distraction and critics19
  • 11.Talk it out — get feedback or company19

Also: keep a bank of old ideas to return to (17), impose deadlines or constraints (15), mine your own life (15), just start (12).

I’ll put it on different speakers. I’ll walk around or do something else. I have this little trampoline in my studio office; I’ll jump on it and do other things. For me, it’s a change of scenery. Whether it’s something super temporary or a small change just in the house or actually shutting the session down.

– Tegan Quin

I just wait. I accept that I might not write anymore. I accept that life changes and that maybe it’s not the end of the world even if it feels like it. That was a thing going on about 15 years ago with me. I don’t really feel that way anymore, but when it was happening it was hard. It feels like it might never come again, and then you just accept it.

– Rickie Lee Jones

Some days I got stuff, some days I don’t, and some days I write about the fact I got nothing to write about. But, I do try to write 1,000 words a day. It’s just like going to the gym. Some workouts are better than others. I think the less pressure you put on yourself, the better. In my opinion, it’d be hard to sit in a room and go, “Okay, damnit. Be creative.

– Henry Rollins

For the actual workload portion of it, I don’t believe in forcing yourself to do work that you don’t like, or that you think is exciting but aren’t ready to do yet. There’s kind of a mantra in journalism that you don’t actually have writer’s block, you’re just not done reporting. I think that that’s true for every kind of writing.

– Kelsey McKinney

Do the worst song you can possibly think of. At the very least, you’ll get some idea of what your rules are. At the most, you’re going to get something that’s better than anything you’ve ever done because it has a lot of pure energy. Just make it bad. Just make it really bad. You know, so pure and bad. I have tried that. That works well.

– Laurie Anderson

On sitting with discomfort — 165 people gave advice on this

Most common: accept not-knowing as a normal — even valuable — part of making things, rather than a problem to solve before you start. Close behind: stay with the feeling instead of rushing to resolve it, and seek discomfort out on purpose.

  • 1.Accept not-knowing and trust the uncertain process35
  • 2.Sit with the feeling instead of rushing to resolve it29
  • 3.Deliberately leave your comfort zone27
  • 4.Reframe fear with a specific mental move23
  • 5.Put the vulnerable, embarrassing material into the work13
  • 6.Persist through hardship that’s part of the job11

Also: self-compassion (9), lean on others (8), treat discomfort as a signal (8), stop caring how you’ll be judged (7), use deadlines to contain open-ended anxiety (5).

Often, when you choose a life of making things, you’re choosing a life where you’re always starting again. You’re also promised nothing....of course, we’re never really promised anything. It’s funny, I find my friends who make things to be the most delusional people, and at the same time, they have what feels like the closest relationship to reality. You have to have some outsized tolerance for not knowing.

– Eliza Callahan

I’m not sure I do something that can be taught. Maybe that sounds really romantic, but I feel like for me education was such a counterproductive experience. Maybe in a few years it’ll be more clear, but I don’t know. What can I say? Something like, “I just collect random phrases and I’m not really sure why, I don’t even remember who wrote them.“ I try to celebrate not knowing.

– Tania Pérez Córdova

You have to be able to sit through the discomfort. And it’s really, really hard.

– Coco Mellors

But I think I’ve learned that if it feels uncomfortable, it’s a good thing and you should lean into it. I’m always trying to put myself in an uncomfortable place because that means that it’s generative; I’m growing and I’m surpassing myself. I don’t think this is the only way to make films, but I don’t know how to make films not from a personal place.

– Ash Goh Hua

When fear takes hold, I ask myself two questions: “Is this true? And so what?“ If I answer those honestly, I can usually break free from it. Also, I have learned to surround myself with people who love and want the best for me. If someone consistently inspires fear or doubt in me, they’re out. As a survivor of narcissistic abuse, this simple boundary has changed my life.

– Orenda Fink

On thinking outside entrenched systems — 174 people gave advice on this

Most common: build your own institution — the gallery, label, venue, school or system you couldn’t get into. The top three are nearly tied and fit together: build your own thing (30), with other people (28), without waiting to be let in (27). Several push back on the lone-DIY version: “it’s not DIY, it’s DIT: do it together.”

  • 1.Build your own institutions, spaces and infrastructure30
  • 2.Rely on community, collaboration and chosen family28
  • 3.Do it yourself — don’t wait for permission27
  • 4.Trust yourself and ignore outside judgment24
  • 5.Protect the work from market pressure21
  • 6.Confront, subvert or route around gatekeepers18
  • 7.Redefine success on your own terms17
  • 8.Treat rest and slowness as resistance14
  • 9.Build financial independence and resourcefulness13

Also: redistribute resources and reject growth for its own sake (8), reach your audience directly (4).

I was never interested in like, “Shut ’em all down, fuck the system,“ it was really like, “We want our own system and you can’t stop us from building it.“ I think there’s no shortage of examples of people who were using the medium of the internet that have done fascinating, brilliant, constructive, independent things. So I certainly don’t think it’s a medium that immediately disqualifies all independent thought.

– Ian MacKaye

“it’s not DIY. It’s DIT: do it together”

– Constanza Valenzuela

No one is going to do it for you, you have to do it yourself. Especially if you want to make content for yourself-you have to do it and find the resources and sacrifice the money and sacrifice your free time and just dive into it. And if it is good stuff that you are making or doing you will find a way to get it to the masses and get funding-or maybe not get funding-but at least insert yourself into a community that will hopefully understand you and support you.

– Eliot Glazer

Yeah. That’s something that’s really stuck with me for the co-op: I would rather have a dope life than a bunch of money. I’m fortunate enough that at this point in my life, we do okay. Well, we do more than okay. I mean, we do great. If you’re a business bro, we don’t do great. But for my standards, we do great.

– Matt Fantastic

While a bit of that is part of the industry, I think it’s also about stepping away from the idea of productivity and reframing what that is because I think not working is productive. I think not doing things is productive. It takes so much mental, emotional, intellectual labor to keep making things. When we realize that there’s a layered type of labor that is occurring to create what we do, I think there’s lots of exhaustion in that and burnout in that.

– Hiba Ali

On getting started — 308 people gave advice on this

By a wide margin: just start — before you feel ready, without a plan, a budget or anyone’s permission, and let the doing teach you. Once you’ve begun, the advice shifts to showing up consistently. (Two halves of the corpus were analyzed separately; they produced the same top two in the same order.)

  • 1.Just start — action beats preparation71
  • 2.Show up consistently; discipline over inspiration51
  • 3.Forge your own path — don’t imitate38
  • 4.Find your people and community36
  • 5.Make peace with failure, rejection and slow timelines33
  • 6.Trust your instincts over outside opinion27
  • 7.Give yourself permission — no one else’s is needed24
  • 8.Do it for love, not approval21
  • 9.Support yourself financially while you build20

Also: pay close attention to the world (16), start small with what you have (15), learn the business side (14), study the craft and the people you admire (13).

Then suddenly you’re at that stage where “Oh, I’ve got to start writing,“ and you’re looking up a very tall mountain. There’s sort of a psychological moment where you have to bite the bullet and just start, and luckily I had a book proposal from which I could start to build out from. On time & spiraling around

Why spiral? We like spirals because they’re about circling back to a core idea over time, something all creative people must do to create whatever it is they’re creating. We think Julia Cameron, in her self-help book The Artist’s Way, puts it best:

“You will circle through some of the issues over and over, each time at a different level. There is no such thing as being done with an artistic life. Frustrations and rewards exist at all levels on the path. Our aim here is to find the trail, establish our footing, and begin the climb.”

Snails (and other gastropods like slugs) excrete slime. They make this slime to move, so that their bodies don’t lose moisture to the rugged terrain beneath them. This slime is beautiful because it glimmers. It’s also beautiful because it’s a map of time recently spent by the snail. Where is the snail now? And where was it going in the first place?

We think the snail is a good mascot for TCI, as it is biologically forced to leave its slime (process, path) everywhere. You could say our goal is to illuminate the (often hidden) slime of the artists we talk to. You could say we are a growing archive of slime. Perhaps it’s a worthy goal, as the snail’s trail doesn’t last long. Even slime evaporates.

– Spiral note from October 14, 2016, by Laurel Schwulst

Ten years in, the single most common piece of advice on this site is also the plainest one. Seventy-one people, asked how to begin, said some version of just start—before you feel ready, before you have the plan or the budget or anyone’s permission. The second most common was to keep showing up after that.

The rest of it is texture: 393 people telling you to go outside, 242 people making work around their kids’ bedtimes, 109 people doing it around a shift, 138 people who would like you to try a particular sandwich. None of that was a plan. It’s just what came up, over and over, when we asked people how they get their work done.
